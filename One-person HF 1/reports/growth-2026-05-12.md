## Growth Regime Verdict

**SLOWING EXPANSION — STAGFLATIONARY DIVERGENCE**

The US economy is sustaining formal expansion (both ISMs above 52, business investment booming) but the internal composition is deeply bifurcated and increasingly stagflationary. Business investment is the standout strength — core capital goods orders +3.29% MoM for the 12th consecutive month — while consumer confidence has collapsed to a historic record low (UMich 48.2, the lowest since tracking began in 1952), housing is contracting, and the labor market is cooling below trend. The most alarming signal is not any single weak indicator but the simultaneous combination of red-hot prices indices (ISM Manufacturing Prices 84.6, ISM Services Prices 70.7) with record pessimism — a stagflationary fingerprint that traps the Fed and historically precedes consumption slowdowns with a 2-3 quarter lag.

## Indicator Deep Dive

### 1. ISM Manufacturing PMI — 52.7 🟢 HEALTHY EXPANSION

- **Latest:** 52.7 | **Prior:** 52.7 | **12-month avg:** ~50.3 | **Benchmark:** 52.0
- **Sub-components:** New Orders: 54.1 (↑ from 53.5) | Production: 53.4 (↓ from 55.1) | Prices: **84.6** ⚠️ (↑ from 78.3 — highest since Apr 2022) | Employment: sub-50 (contraction)
- **Interpretation:** Manufacturing is unambiguously in expansion for the 18th consecutive month, with the PMI-to-GDP model implying ~1.8% annualized real GDP growth — but the 25.6-point surge in the Prices index over three months to its highest level since April 2022 is a screaming stagflation alarm. Companies are paying more for inputs and employment is contracting within the sector, signaling margin compression and reluctance to hire even as orders grow.
- **Trend:** Stable — hovering at 52.7 for consecutive months, neither accelerating nor rolling over. New Orders sub-index turning higher is the one positive leading signal.

### 2. ISM Services PMI — 53.6 🟢 HEALTHY EXPANSION

- **Latest:** 53.6 | **Prior (March):** 54.0 | **12-month avg:** ~53.4 | **Benchmark:** 52.0
- **Sub-components:** Business Activity: 55.9 (↑ from 53.9) | New Orders: 53.5 (↓ sharply from 60.6) | Prices: **70.7** ⚠️ (highest since Oct 2022) | Employment: **48.0** — contraction for second straight month
- **Interpretation:** Services remains in expansion (22nd consecutive month) and drives the GDP read (~1.7% annualized), but the internals are deteriorating in ways not visible in the headline. Services employment is now in contraction — the sector that employs ~80% of US workers is shedding positions. New Orders fell 7.1 points MoM, the sharpest single-month deceleration in over a year. The Prices index at 70.7 is the second-highest in four years. This is expansion with a softening underbelly.
- **Trend:** Deteriorating slowly — headline above 52 but employment and new orders pointing to deceleration ahead.

### 3. UMich Consumer Sentiment — 48.2 🔴 RECESSION-LEVEL PESSIMISM

- **Latest:** 48.2 (May preliminary) | **Prior (April final):** 49.8 | **Long-run avg:** ~86 | **Benchmark floor:** 75
- **1-year inflation expectations:** 4.5% (down from 4.7%) | **5-year inflation expectations:** 3.4% (down from 3.5%)
- **Interpretation:** 48.2 is not just below the 75 floor — it is a *record all-time low since tracking began in 1952*. The survey director specifically cited surging gasoline prices (~1/3 of respondents) and tariffs (~30% of respondents) as the primary drivers. Historically, UMich readings below 60 have coincided with or immediately preceded recession. The fact that 5-year inflation expectations remain at 3.4% (well above the Fed's 2% target) is critical: de-anchored long-run expectations reduce the Fed's ability to cut rates even as growth softens. This is the single most alarming data point in the dataset.
- **Fed signal:** 1-year at 4.5% and 5-year at 3.4% with *record consumer pessimism* is the classic stagflation combination. The Fed cannot credibly ease into these inflation expectations.

### 4. Building Permits — 1.37M SAAR 🔴 BELOW TREND

- **Latest (March 2026):** 1.372M SAAR (↓10.8% MoM, ↓7.4% YoY) | **Prior (Feb):** 1.538M | **Benchmark:** 1.50M
- **Single-family:** 895K (↓3.8% MoM — just below the 900K healthy threshold) | **Multi-family:** 427K (sharp decline)
- **Note:** April 2026 data scheduled for release May 21 — not yet available.
- **Interpretation:** The March data is a sharp reversal — permits fell 10.8% MoM after a strong February. Single-family permits at 895K are just clinging below the 900K healthy threshold. Multi-family (apartments) collapsed. Since housing leads the economic cycle by 6-12 months, a sustained decline in permits signals construction employment and residential investment weakness through late 2026 into 2027. The current mortgage rate environment (~7%+) is suppressing demand; permits are declining *despite* unmet housing supply needs, which confirms demand destruction rather than supply constraints.
- **Lead indicator:** If permits remain at this level (1.30-1.40M) through Q2, expect housing starts to follow down in H2 2026.

- **Unemployment rate:** 4.3% (unchanged) | **Avg hourly earnings:** +0.2% MoM / +3.6% YoY | **Labor force participation:** Not specified in release

Manufacturing payrolls declining (-2K) and information shedding jobs (-13K) are leading indicators of the industrial deceleration visible in IP and raw materials data. Healthcare and transport adding jobs reflects defensive/resilient sectors, not cyclical strength. Wage growth at +3.6% YoY is sticky — above what's needed for Fed comfort on inflation.
- **Labor market trend:** Cooling — the 3-month average is now ~140K vs. 200K+ a year ago. Not a collapse, but a sustained drift below trend.

### 6. Durable Goods Orders — Core +3.29% MoM 🟢 STRONG

- **Headline orders MoM:** +0.8% (following -1.2% in February)
- **Core capital goods (ex-defense, ex-aircraft) MoM:** **+3.29%** — highest since June 2020
- **Core shipments MoM:** +1.2% | **Core shipments YoY:** +7.0%
- **12-consecutive months of core capital goods gains** | **Core orders YoY:** +6.7%
- **Benchmark:** Flat to +0.5% MoM
- **Interpretation:** This is the strongest component in the dataset by far and a genuine positive divergence. Core capital goods orders (the cleanest proxy for business investment in equipment) have now grown for 12 straight months, are up 6.7% YoY, and the March print was the strongest since June 2020. This is overwhelmingly driven by AI-related infrastructure capex — data center equipment, semiconductors, advanced manufacturing tooling. The SMH +52% YTD and Fortinet/Synopsys demand commentary are consistent with this. Business investment is the one sector that is genuinely accelerating, not decelerating.
- **Caveat:** Defense aircraft orders inflated the headline — core is the right measure, and even core may reflect a few large AI data center orders. The breadth of gains across 12 months reduces the one-order distortion risk.

### 7. Industrial Production / Capacity Utilization — +1.4% YoY / 76.1% 🔴 / 🟡

- **Industrial Production MoM:** +0.7% (after -0.3% in March) | **YoY:** +1.4% | **Benchmark:** +2.0% YoY
- **Manufacturing output MoM:** +0.6% | Mining: -0.1% | Utilities: +1.9%
- **Capacity Utilization:** 76.1% (↑ from 75.5%) — **3.3 percentage points below long-run average** | **Benchmark:** 78–82%
- **Manufacturing capacity utilization:** 75.8% — 2.4 percentage points below long-run average
- **Interpretation:** Industrial production is recovering (+0.7% MoM) but the YoY rate of +1.4% remains below the 2.0% expansion threshold. Capacity utilization at 76.1% confirms meaningful slack in the industrial economy — factories are not running near full capacity, which implies limited pricing power for industrial goods producers and no inflationary pressure from the supply side of goods production. This slack, combined with the ISM Prices spike, suggests the inflation is being driven by imported costs (tariffs, energy) rather than domestic capacity tightness — which makes it stagflationary rather than demand-pull.
- **Divergence flag:** IP YoY at +1.4% is inconsistent with core durable goods orders +6.7% YoY — the gap likely reflects the lag between orders and actual production, and may mean IP accelerates in Q3 2026 as AI capex orders convert to manufacturing activity.

## Divergences and Cross-Checks

**Critical divergence — ISMs vs. Consumer Sentiment:**
Both ISMs are above 52 and signaling healthy expansion; UMich is at a record all-time low. This is a textbook stagflation divergence: businesses are ordering and producing, but consumers are miserable because prices are surging. The ISM Prices indices (Manufacturing: 84.6, Services: 70.7) explain the gap — cost pressure is flowing through to consumers at the gas pump and grocery store, destroying sentiment even as the aggregate economy registers "expansion." This divergence historically resolves in one of two ways: (1) price pressures ease and sentiment recovers, or (2) sentiment collapse leads to consumption cuts that drag ISMs below 50 with a 2-3 quarter lag.

**NFP vs. Consumer Sentiment:**
+115K jobs added in April, unemployment at 4.3% — yet consumer sentiment is at an all-time low. Consumers have jobs but feel poorer because inflation is eroding real purchasing power (wage growth +3.6% YoY vs. UMich 1-year inflation expectations 4.5% = negative real wage perception). This is a dangerous combination: employment is holding up, which removes the Fed's primary justification for cuts, while consumers feel worse than at any point in modern history.

**Core Durable Goods (+3.29%) vs. IP YoY (+1.4%) — Positive divergence:**
Orders are surging (+6.7% YoY) but production is only +1.4% YoY. This is a forward-looking positive: the IP data should accelerate over the next 2-3 quarters as AI capex orders convert to manufactured output. Industrial production is likely to be revised higher and to accelerate once the AI infrastructure buildout hits manufacturing capacity.

**Raw Materials (MT/AA/DOW declining) vs. IP recovering — Structural shift warning:**
The divergence between recovering IP and declining raw material company revenue/earnings suggests the US industrial economy is becoming less material-intensive. AI data centers, semiconductor fabs, and advanced manufacturing consume more energy, silicon, and software than steel, aluminum, and basic chemicals per dollar of output. This secular shift is real and will continue to weight against heavy materials producers.

**Building Permits vs. Durable Goods Strength:**
Housing permits are collapsing (-10.8% MoM) while business capex is booming. These are telling opposite stories about different parts of the economy: households are not investing in housing (rate-constrained), but businesses are investing aggressively in equipment (AI-driven, relatively rate-insensitive because the ROI case is compelling). GDP growth can be sustained by business investment even as residential investment falls — but the consumer-facing weakness will eventually limit overall growth.

## Macro Implication for Portfolio

**Growth regime implication:** The US is in a *bifurcated slowing expansion* — not a recession, not a clean expansion. Business investment is the one genuine tailwind; consumer spending is under severe pressure. This argues for **quality cyclicals over broad cyclicals**, specifically the intersection of technology/AI infrastructure and defense (both benefit from the business investment cycle and are insulated from consumer weakness). Avoid rate-sensitive sectors (homebuilders, REITs, consumer discretionary) given both the housing permit data and the stagflationary macro backdrop.

**Fed policy implication:** The Fed is trapped. NFP at +115K is below trend but not catastrophic (unemployment at 4.3%). UMich inflation expectations at 4.5% (1-year) and 3.4% (5-year) with ISM Prices at decade highs make rate cuts politically and empirically indefensible. The base case is no cuts in 2026, with the risk skewed toward a delayed or cancelled easing cycle. Any rate cut in this environment risks de-anchoring inflation expectations and would likely produce a bond market selloff, not a rally.

**Sector tilts implied by this specific combination:**
- **Overweight:** AI/semiconductor capex names (confirmed by core durable goods +3.29% for 12th straight month), defense (RTX, NOC backlogs growing, government spending durable), healthcare (defensive hiring in NFP, resilient revenue)
- **Neutral:** Large-cap consumer staples (revenue growth is inflation-driven, not volume-driven; expensive on PE if volume growth stalls)
- **Underweight:** Homebuilders and REITs (housing permits collapsing, rates unlikely to fall), consumer discretionary (UMich record low = consumer under severe stress), raw materials / heavy industrials (MT/AA/DOW revenue declining, capacity utilization slack, no pricing power on domestic goods)
- **Watch:** If the UMich divergence from ISMs persists for another 1-2 months, the scenario becomes a leading indicator of ISM rollover below 50 with a 2-quarter lag — which would be a full slowing signal requiring a risk-off rotation into quality and defensives.
